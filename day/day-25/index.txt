<div class="vc"><div class="hz">要</div><div class="meta py">yào · Modal &amp; Mental Verbs · <span class="mono">W.253</span></div><div class="en">to want; will; need to</div></div>
<div class="vc"><div class="hz">不要</div><div class="meta py">bùyào · Modal &amp; Mental Verbs · <span class="mono">W.014</span> · spoken <span class="py">búyào</span></div><div class="en">don’t; must not</div></div>

<div class="card learn"><div class="exbody"><b>Examples:</b> <span class="hz">我要茶。</span> <span class="py">Wǒ yào chá.</span> · <span class="hz">我要去饭店。</span> <span class="py">Wǒ yào qù fàndiàn.</span> · <span class="hz">我不要牛奶。</span> <span class="py">Wǒ bùyào niúnǎi.</span><br/>
<b><span class="hz">要</span> is the most direct verb you have met.</b> <span class="hz">想</span> from Day 17 says what you would like; <span class="hz">要</span> says what you want, and in a shop or a restaurant it is the normal, unremarkable way to order.<br/>
<b><span class="hz">不要</span> is written with <span class="hz">不</span> but said with a rise.</b> <span class="hz">要</span> is fourth tone, so the <span class="hz">不</span> shifts to <span class="py">bú</span> — the Day-3 rule, applied to a word you now own. You write <span class="py">bùyào</span> and you say <span class="py">búyào</span>.<br/>
<b>VI-10 usability:</b> all twelve are freely usable today. Both new grammar points land in this lesson, and everything else rests on the verb-object basics from Day 5 and the adjective pattern from Day 4.</div></div>
<div class="warn"><b>Near-pair contrasts, taught explicitly (VI-7).</b><br/>
<b>① <span class="hz">要</span> vs <span class="hz">想</span>.</b> <span class="hz">我想喝茶。</span> = “I’d like some tea” — a wish. <span class="hz">我要茶。</span> = “I’ll have tea” — an order. Both are polite in a restaurant; the second is what you actually say to the person taking it down.<br/>
<b>② <span class="hz">不要</span> vs <span class="hz">不想</span>.</b> <span class="hz">我不想吃。</span> = “I don’t feel like eating.” <span class="hz">我不要。</span> = “No thanks” / “I don’t want it.” And <span class="hz">不要</span> can also tell someone else not to do something, which <span class="hz">不想</span> never does.<br/>
<b>③ <span class="hz">吃</span> vs <span class="hz">喝</span>.</b> Chinese splits them exactly as English does — you eat solids and drink liquids — with one thing to watch: soup is eaten, not drunk, in Chinese. That is beyond HSK 1, but it is why the two verbs are never interchangeable.<br/>
<b>④ <span class="hz">饭</span> vs <span class="hz">菜</span> vs <span class="hz">饭店</span>.</b> <span class="hz">饭</span> is the rice or the meal as a whole; <span class="hz">菜</span> is the individual dishes; <span class="hz">饭店</span> is the building. <span class="hz">我在饭店吃饭</span> uses two of them; the third arrives whenever you name what is actually on the table.<br/>
<b>⑤ <span class="hz">水</span> and <span class="hz">水</span>-words.</b> <span class="hz">水</span> is third tone, <span class="py">shuǐ</span>, and the <span class="mono">-ui</span> is the contracted final from Day 16 — the mark sits on the last letter.</div>10:T129c,<p class="lead">Two points, and they are <b>sequenced rather than merely ordered</b>: G48 is built on G47, so G47 is taught first and G48 refers back to it. This same-day dependency is declared in the contract (G-03).</p>
<div class="card learn">
<div class="exhead"><span class="pill">G47</span><span class="exname">要 (want / need / will)</span><span class="extitle">difficulty: core · prerequisite: G14 (Day 5) · <b>taught first — G48 depends on it</b></span></div>
<div class="exbody">
<p><b>Notice first.</b> One word, two jobs. Put a noun after <span class="hz">要</span> and it means “want”. Put a verb after it and it means “will” or “need to”. Chinese does not distinguish them; the thing that follows decides.</p>
<p><b>The rule.</b> <span class="hz">要</span> sits where any verb sits — after the subject, before what it governs.</p>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">Subject + 要 + Noun  (want) ·  Subject + 要 + Verb (+ Object)  (will / need to)</div><div class="ex">我要茶。 / 我要一个杯子。 / 我要去饭店。 / 我要吃饭。</div></div>
<p><b>Two examples:</b></p>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">我要茶，他要水。</div><div class="ex"><span class="py">Wǒ yào chá, tā yào shuǐ.</span> “I’ll have tea, he’ll have water.” — the ordering use, with a noun.</div></div>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">我要去饭店吃饭。</div><div class="ex"><span class="py">Wǒ yào qù fàndiàn chī fàn.</span> “I’m going to go to the restaurant to eat.” — with a verb, stacked on Day 19’s serial pattern without any change.</div></div>
<p><b>Minimal contrast (GE-4):</b> <span class="hz">我要茶。</span> ✓ vs <span class="hz">我想茶。</span> ✗ — <span class="hz">想</span> cannot take a bare noun object this way; it wants a verb. That is the cleanest structural difference between the two words. And <span class="hz">我要吃饭。</span> and <span class="hz">我想吃饭。</span> are both correct and mean different things: the first is a plan, the second a wish.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Measure words still apply.</b> <span class="hz">我要一个杯子。</span> takes <span class="hz">个</span>, exactly as it would after any other verb. <span class="hz">要</span> changes nothing about how the noun phrase is built.</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="card learn">
<div class="exhead"><span class="pill">G48</span><span class="exname">不要 (don’t / don’t want)</span><span class="extitle">difficulty: core · prerequisites: <b>G47 (today, taught above)</b> · G2 (Day 1)</span></div>
<div class="exbody">
<p><b>Notice first.</b> Now that <span class="hz">要</span> has two jobs, its negation has two too. <span class="hz">不要 + Noun</span> refuses a thing. <span class="hz">不要 + Verb</span> tells someone not to do something.</p>
<p><b>The rule.</b> Put <span class="hz">不</span> in front of <span class="hz">要</span>, exactly as you have negated every other verb since Day 1. Nothing else moves.</p>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">Subject + 不要 + Noun  (refuse) ·  不要 + Verb  (don’t do it)</div><div class="ex">我不要牛奶。 / 我不要茶，我要水。 / 不要吃这个菜。</div></div>
<p><b>Two examples:</b></p>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">我不要牛奶，我要水。</div><div class="ex"><span class="py">Wǒ bùyào niúnǎi, wǒ yào shuǐ.</span> Said <span class="py">Wǒ búyào niúnǎi…</span> — refusing one thing and naming another is the standard shape of a correction in a restaurant.</div></div>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">这个菜不好吃，不要吃。</div><div class="ex"><span class="py">Zhège cài bù hǎochī, bùyào chī.</span> Said <span class="py">…búyào chī.</span> “This dish isn’t good, don’t eat it.” — the instruction use, with no subject at all.</div></div>
<p><b>Minimal contrast (GE-4):</b> <span class="hz">我不要茶。</span> ✓ = I don’t want tea, vs <span class="hz">我不想喝茶。</span> ✓ = I don’t feel like drinking tea. Both correct, different force — the first refuses an offer, the second reports a mood. And when the second syllable is <span class="hz">要</span>, the sound shifts: <span class="py">búyào</span>, never <span class="py">bùyào</span>.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Why this needed G47 first.</b> <span class="hz">不要</span> is not an independent word you could learn cold — it is <span class="hz">要</span> under negation, and both of its meanings follow from the two meanings of <span class="hz">要</span>. <div class="exhead"><span class="exname">Track item D25 · what a whole sentence does, on top of its tones</span><span class="extitle">drill material from taught vocabulary only (PT-4)</span></div>
<p><b>Every syllable still carries its own tone.</b> That does not change. What sits on top of the tones is the shape of the whole sentence — and Chinese does this differently from English, in a way that catches people who expect questions to rise.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Sentence</th><th>Type</th><th>What the whole line does</th></tr>
<tr><td class="hz">你要茶。 <span class="py">Nǐ yào chá.</span></td><td>statement</td><td>settles downward; the last syllable ends low</td></tr>
<tr><td class="hz">你要茶吗？ <span class="py">Nǐ yào chá ma?</span></td><td class="mono">吗</td><td>stays level, lifts <b>only slightly</b> on <span class="hz">吗</span> — the particle does the asking, not the pitch</td></tr>
<tr><td class="hz">这个菜好吃。 <span class="py">Zhège cài hǎochī.</span></td><td>statement</td><td>settles; <span class="py">chī</span> keeps its high level tone but the line falls away</td></tr>
<tr><td class="hz">这个菜好吃吗？ <span class="py">Zhège cài hǎochī ma?</span></td><td class="mono">吗</td><td>the tones are identical to the statement; only <span class="hz">吗</span> is added</td></tr>
</table>
<p><b>This is the key point.</b> In English, “You want tea.” and “You want tea?” differ <em>only</em> in pitch. In Chinese they differ by a word. <span class="hz">吗</span> is doing the work, so the tones underneath stay exactly where they were — and if you swing the whole sentence upward the way English does, you distort the tones and become harder to understand, not easier.</p>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">你要茶。 —— 你要茶吗？</div><div class="ex"><b>Say it:</b> the two lines should sound almost the same. The only differences are the extra light syllable and a slight lift at the very end. Alternate them four times and try to keep <span class="py">chá</span> identical in both.</div></div>
<div class="grule"><div class="pat">这个菜很好吃。 —— 这个菜好吃吗？ —— 你喝水。 —— 你喝水吗？</div><div class="ex"><b>Say it:</b> four lines, two pairs. Record yourself and check that the third tone in <span class="py">shuǐ</span> is a genuine dip in <em>both</em> members of the pair.</div></div>
<div class="note"><b>A small licence at the end.</b> A real speaker does lift a little on <span class="hz">吗</span> — that is natural and you should copy it. What you must not do is start the lift early and drag the whole sentence up with it. Keep the rise on the last light syllable and leave the tones alone.</div>
